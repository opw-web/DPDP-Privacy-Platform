0 · What this is, and why it exists

Read this part once, the day before. You are not memorising it — you are making sure that when someone asks "but what is it for?", you have an answer that does not begin with a technical word.

The law, in plain English

In 2023 India passed the Digital Personal Data Protection Act. In 2025 the rules that go with it were notified. Together they say something simple, and quite demanding:

If your company holds information about a person, that person has rights over it — and you have to be able to prove you respected them.

The Act gives two things names, and it is worth using them, because everything in this software is built around the pair:

Data Principal

The person the data is about. You. Me. A customer. The law puts them at the centre and gives them rights.

Data Fiduciary

The company holding that data. Not an "owner" — a fiduciary, someone trusted to look after something that belongs to someone else.

That word choice is the whole point. A company does not own your phone number. It is holding it on trust, for a reason you were told about, for as long as that reason lasts.

What the Act then demands of that company:

  • Tell people, before you take it. In clear language, itemised — not "we may collect certain information".
  • Have a lawful reason for every use. Either the person consented, or the law lists your specific situation. Nothing else counts.
  • Make withdrawing as easy as agreeing. If saying yes took one click, saying no cannot take a phone call and a form.
  • Answer people who ask. What do you hold about me? Correct it. Erase it. Within a deadline, not eventually.
  • Protect children by default. No tracking, no targeted advertising at under-18s. A parent's permission does not unlock it.
  • Report a breach immediately. Tell every affected person, and tell the Data Protection Board — the initial report at once, the detailed one within 72 hours of finding out.
  • Do not keep it forever. When the reason ends, the data goes — but never before a statutory minimum.
  • Be able to prove all of the above. Not describe it. Prove it, with records.

The penalty for getting it badly wrong runs to ₹250 crore for a single failure to protect data.

So what does this software actually do?

Here is the problem it solves, in one picture. A mid-sized Indian retailer does not have "a customer database". It has four or five of them, bought at different times, run by different teams:

  • The marketing team's mailing tool, where you are customer_email.
  • The sales team's CRM, where you are primary_email.
  • The support desk, where you are user_ref.
  • The online shop, where you are customer_code.

You are one person. To that company you are four rows, spelled four different ways, and nobody has ever tried to line them up. So when you write in and say "tell me everything you hold about me", the honest answer is that they do not know. And the Act gives them a deadline to find out.

This platform is what sits on top of those systems and makes the company able to answer. It:

  1. Reads the existing systems, without ever writing back to them.
  2. Works out which rows are the same human being — and, crucially, refuses to guess when it is not sure.
  3. Records why the company is allowed to hold each thing, and who else it was given to.
  4. Tells each person what is held, in their language, and lets them agree or refuse purpose by purpose.
  5. Handles their requests against deadlines the company configured from the actual rules — not deadlines a programmer typed into the code.
  6. Runs the clock when there is a breach, and produces the reports the regulator asks for.
  7. Records everything in a log that cannot be quietly edited afterwards.
Worth saying out loud

Installing software does not make a company compliant, and this platform never claims otherwise. It does the part software can do: find the data, keep the record, run the clock, and produce the evidence. The judgement calls stay with a human, and the platform is careful to ask for them rather than invent them.

The one-paragraph version

Say this

"India's data protection law says a company has to know what personal data it holds, why it is allowed to hold it, and who it shared it with — and has to prove it. Most companies genuinely cannot answer that, because the same customer sits in four different systems under four different spellings. This platform reads those systems without changing them, works out who is who, and then runs everything the law requires on top: the notices, the consent, the requests, the deadlines, the breach clock, and an audit log nobody can edit afterwards. Let me show you."

1 · From a cold laptop to the app on screen

Roughly four minutes, almost all of it waiting. You will double-click one icon and watch a window fill with sentences.

Before the audience arrives

  1. Switch the laptop on and log in as normal.
  2. Close anything you do not need. The demo uses a web browser and nothing else.
  3. Go to the Desktop — minimise everything, or press the show-desktop key. The eight buttons are there; they are also in the folder the platform was unzipped into.
  4. Start Docker Desktop if it is not already running, and wait until it says Engine running. Button 1 checks for it and stops rather than half-starting.

The eight buttons

There are eight controls, numbered in the order you use them. Numbering is deliberate: you never have to remember which is which, only what comes next. Button 0 is the one-time setup and will already have been run on a demonstration laptop.

2 · The demonstration

Twelve acts, roughly ninety seconds each. Every screen already has real data in it — nothing here asks you to fill in a form while people watch. Each act tells you what to click, what will appear, a line you can say, and which part of the law it answers.

How to read an act

Do is the click. You will see is what should appear — if it does not, you have the wrong page. Say is a line you can use as-is or reword. In law is there for the question that always comes.

Telling people what you hold

"The Act does not accept 'we may collect certain information'. It wants the list, itemised. So this notice is not typed by hand — it is generated from the field mappings you saw in the first screen. If the company starts collecting date of birth tomorrow, the notice knows."

Try to edit the published version.

You will see

It refuses. You can only create a new version.

"Once published, it is frozen — the database itself refuses the edit, not just the screen. Guard against the obvious question

If asked "can he see anyone else's data?" — no, and it is structural rather than a filter someone remembered to write. Every database query in the system is scoped to the signed-in identity at a level below the application code.

8

Consent, and taking it back

~2 min
Do

Open Consents on the staff side.

You will see

A dashboard per purpose: granted, refused, withdrawn, and not yet asked.

Say

"Consent here is not one switch. It is per purpose. You can agree to order updates and refuse marketing, and the system holds both at once — which is exactly what the Act requires and what most cookie banners quietly refuse to do."

Then do

Click into a purpose and open one consent record.

Say

"And every record is frozen to the exact version of the notice the person was shown at that moment. Not the current wording — the wording on the day. Otherwise the company is claiming consent to something the person never read."

Then do

Point out the withdrawn entry (Neha Rao), then open Retention.

Say

"Neha withdrew her consent. One click, exactly as many as it took to give it — the Act is explicit that withdrawing cannot be harder than agreeing. And the moment she did, the system created an erasure task on its own."

Then, pointing at the deferral

"But look — it has not run. It is deferred, with a release date and a citation. Because there is a rule that says certain records must be kept for at least a year. So the law pushes in both directions at once, and the platform holds both: erase this, but not yet, and here is exactly why."

Then, pointing at the state filters

"Every task sits in a state you can point at — evaluated, notice sent, deferred, on legal hold, ready, erased, cancelled. Nothing is a silent failure. And before anything is erased, the person gets the forty-eight hour notice the rules require. If they sign in during that window, the task is cancelled under Rule 8(2) instead. Both of those are scheduled jobs, so neither depends on somebody remembering."

In law

Spec steps 15 to 18, 29 and 30. Section 6(4) and 6(6) — withdrawal as easy as giving, and consequences follow. Rule 8(2) — the pre-erasure notice and its cancellation. Rule 8(3) — the retention floor.

Acme is marked a Significant Data Fiduciary, so this page carries the extra duties: the assessment and audit cycle (its length comes from the configured rule, never a constant in the code), the register of algorithms that operate on personal data with their risk reviews and mitigations, and the localisation gaps.

Say, if asked: "It flags this for a human. It never blocks a transfer and never marks a review as done by itself — which is the honest behaviour, because neither of those is a decision software should be making."

The processors, sharing activities, cross-border transfers, retention policies and security measures behind everything else. This is where the "who has my data" answer in Act 6 comes from.

Appendix C · Rebuilding from zero

Button 9 destroys everything and builds it again from the four source systems. You do not need it for a normal demo — but it is the most convincing thing in the box, so it is worth knowing when to use it.

What it actually does

It asks you to confirm, then works through nine steps, narrating each one:

  1. Stops the running app.
  2. Throws the database away and rebuilds it from the project's own migrations.
  3. Reseeds the four demo source systems with their fixed dataset.
  4. Rebuilds and restarts everything.
  5. Connects all four systems through the real API, maps the fields, and synchronises them one at a time.
  6. Completes the compliance seed — notices and portal accounts.
  7. Records the one governance decision the accuracy dashboard needs from a human.
  8. Prints the numbers it actually observed.
  9. Fills in the demo data: the notices, requests, consents, guardian, campaigns, breach, SDF declaration and Government request.

Nothing in there is a shortcut. Every artifact is created through the same API the website uses, and if the platform would refuse a human doing it by hand, it refuses the script too.

Which of the two to do

The night before — recommended

Press 9 the evening before, let it finish, check the numbers, then press 6. On the day you press 1 and you are ready in two minutes. This is the default and it is what this runbook assumes.

Live, as the opening act

Genuinely impressive — they watch 500 rows become 327 people from an empty database. But you are standing there for twelve minutes. Only do this if you have the time and the room is patient.

How to tell it worked

When it finishes, step 8 prints the numbers. They should read:

500
Raw records
327
Principals
4
Pending review
12
Conflicts
6
Under-18

Then step 9 prints a checklist of everything it staged, and finally it signs in to the public portal for real, to prove that door opens. If any line in that checklist says [FAILED], a screen in the demo will be empty. Run button 9 again before you rely on it.

One known wobble, stated plainly

The principal count has occasionally come out as 328 rather than 327 across resets. The reset script prints what it actually found rather than what it hoped for, and warns about this. If you see 328, the demo still works — but say the number on the screen, not the number in this document.

The one rule

Never press button 9 during a demo. It destroys everything and takes twelve minutes. Every other button is safe at any time.

If something goes wrong mid-sentence

Say what happened and move on. This is compliance software: an audience that has just watched you explain that the platform refuses to guess, refuses to send an incomplete breach notice, and refuses to let a company quietly edit a published notice, will think considerably more of you for saying "that screen has not loaded, let me come back to it" than for improvising. The whole argument of the demo is that honest software beats confident software. Be the same.
